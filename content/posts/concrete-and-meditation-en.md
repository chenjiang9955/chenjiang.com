Twelve years in real estate finance. When collections hit twenty billion, I was staring at spreadsheets. When tax refunds exceeded a hundred million, I was arguing with tax officials. Excel, PowerPoint, financial models—these are the languages I know best.

But there are moments when the numbers stop working.

One late night, walking out of the office tower, I looked up at the skyline dotted with lights. These buildings—I contributed to them. Capital planning, budgeting, tax structuring—I was involved in every phase. Yet standing among these things I helped create, what I felt wasn't pride. It was a strange kind of emptiness.

As if I'd spent my life building outward, and forgotten to look inward.

## The Efficiency Trap

Real estate worships efficiency. Collection rates, profit margins, IRR, ROE—every metric demands faster, higher, stronger. Immersed in this culture for twelve years, you unconsciously internalize its logic. You start managing your life like a project, measuring happiness with KPIs, evaluating relationships with ROI.

But life isn't a project. Projects have endpoints. Life doesn't—or rather, its endpoint isn't "completion" but "experience."

This is probably why I turned to Buddhism. Not to escape reality, but to enter it more clearly. Meditation isn't about becoming passive. It's about waking from the hypnosis of efficiency and seeing what's actually driving you.

## What's Pushing You

"All conditioned things are impermanent"—the first time I read these words, I didn't feel shock. I felt relief.

Real estate is intensely cyclical. When the market is up, everyone believes it will stay up forever. When it crashes, everyone believes it's the end. But zoom out ten, twenty years, and the alternation between boom and bust is almost inevitable. Impermanence isn't the disaster. Mistaking a temporary phase for permanence is. The pain comes from grasping at the "good times" as if they'd last, then being blindsided when they don't.

This applies personally too. We treat "who I am now" as a fixed entity and exhaust ourselves defending it. But that self—yesterday's me, last year's me, the rookie cashier twelve years ago—has already flowed away through countless causes and conditions. Much of our anxiety comes from running hard for a fixed self that doesn't actually exist.

## Awareness Doesn't Need a Mountain

Many think practice requires retreating to mountains, eating vegetarian, sitting for hours. I don't have those conditions. And I don't need them.

The real practice field is daily life. The flash of anger when someone challenges you in a meeting. The wave of resentment at 2 AM working overtime. The shapeless anxiety before year-end reviews. Each moment an emotion rises is a training opportunity. Not to suppress it, but to *see* it.

See anger rising. Name it: "Ah, this is anger." Then observe. Where is it? Tight chest? Shallow breath? What are the thoughts saying? Just watch. Don't do anything. Watching, it will fade on its own—impermanence. No emotion lasts forever.

This act of seeing is awareness. Awareness is both incredibly simple and incredibly difficult. Simple enough to take one second. Difficult enough that we'll spend a lifetime forgetting to do it.

## Building an Invisible Structure

I've helped build many buildings. But what I really want to build is an invisible structure—a heart that can see itself clearly and rest in change.

This building needs no planning permit, no capital planning, no construction milestones. It only needs a few minutes of quiet each day. It only needs pulling yourself back, again and again, from the flood of thoughts. It only needs saying to anxiety when it rises: I know you're anxiety. It's okay. Have a seat. I'll sit with you for a while.

This building will never be finished. That's okay. Because the process of building it is the process of living in it.
