Time changes when you travel alone.

In ordinary life, a day is cut into pieces by meetings, spreadsheets, messages, and small obligations. On the road, the day suddenly becomes whole. You do not need to rush between two points. You do not need to handle three things at once. You do not need to be ready to reply "received" at every moment.

You only need to walk. To be here. To look.

This state of "just being" feels to me like a form of meditation.

## Leaving the Frame

We live inside countless frames. A job title is a frame: finance manager, city head. A relationship is a frame: someone's subordinate, someone's colleague. A city is a frame too: Shenzhen's speed, anxiety, and ambition.

These frames are not false. They do define part of us. But they also confine us when we mistake them for the whole.

The precious thing about travel is that it briefly removes you from these frames. In an unfamiliar city, no one knows what you do, no one carries assumptions about your past, and no one expects you to behave in a fixed way. This state of having no identity gives you a chance to touch the self outside the frame.

What is that self like? It may be quieter, softer, or more uncertain. Whatever it is, it is closer to truth than the self trapped inside a role.

## The Texture of Solitude

Traveling alone makes solitude necessary. In a strange place, you cannot always pick up the phone to complain to a friend, go home to a partner, or ask a colleague to lunch. Your feelings have to pass through you.

That sounds lonely. In some sense it is. But this loneliness is not empty. It is full, like a glass of water left still until the sediment slowly settles and the water becomes clear.

I remember sitting one evening in the courtyard of a small inn in Yunnan. In front of me were layered mountains and a sky gradually darkening. There was no phone signal, no work email, nothing that needed a response. At that moment I suddenly felt the absurdity of it: I had spent more than thirty years before allowing myself to sit down and watch a complete sunset.

It was not that I never had the chance. I simply never stopped.

The religion of efficiency makes you feel that stopping is a waste of time, and wasting time is a sin. But what exactly are we rushing toward?

## Walking as Meditation

Walking is a strange and beautiful thing. If the body moves and the mind keeps moving too - calculating distance, worrying about weather, thinking of the next destination - then it is only transit.

But if attention returns to walking itself - the contact of the sole with the ground, the rhythm of breath, the temperature of wind on the skin - walking becomes meditation.

Walking meditation needs no special place. Any road can hold it: an airport corridor, a train platform, an unfamiliar stone lane. You do not need to close your eyes. You only need to bring attention back from "where am I going?" to "I am walking."

This may be the deeper reason I like traveling alone. Travel gives me permission to slow down, to simply be, and to accomplish nothing.

## Returning

Every journey ends in return. But a good journey means that the person who returns is no longer exactly the person who left.

Not because of the scenery. Not because of a place checked off a list. But because in those slow, solitary moments when familiar frames stop working, you come a little closer to yourself.

You realize that many things that made you anxious were not as heavy as they seemed. You realize that the person you have been trying to become may be the one walking alone on a strange street, with nothing to prove.

The essence of travel is not departure. It is return.

Not escape from life, but a clearer return to it.
