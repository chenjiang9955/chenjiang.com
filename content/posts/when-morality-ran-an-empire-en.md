## A Land Dispute

A land dispute is brought before Hai Rui.

When the evidence alone could not settle a case, Hai Rui had a set of principles of his own. In disputes among brothers, uncles, and nephews, he gave greater weight to seniority; in suits between local gentry and poor commoners, he tended to protect the weaker side. Kinship cases were judged through ethical order, disputes over property with an eye to relative strength. The same official was at once an administrator, a judge, and a moral arbiter for local society.

It would be too easy to call such a judgment foolish or absurd. Hai Rui was not careless. If anything, he was among the civil officials most serious about putting his beliefs into practice. He believed in ethical order and was willing to bear a personal cost for it. Yet a land dispute involved contracts, property rights, fact-finding, and the weighing of evidence—matters for which there was no sufficiently fine-grained institutional framework. The most dependable and familiar instrument in an official’s hands was, in the end, morality.

Behind a land dispute there was usually far more than a single contract. Rural landholdings were dispersed; rights to them might cross several generations of families. Good years and bad years repeatedly produced loans, pawns, mortgages, and transfers. Determining who truly owned a plot could involve several contracts, several debts, and a tangle of kinship relations.

The government of the time did not possess the organizational resources or technical capacity to investigate every one of those relations and then make a precise judgment from the facts. What the magistrate’s decision cared about was not necessarily whether every item received a strict and equivalent fairness. It was whether the ruling could preserve the broad ethical order, persuade both sides to accept the result, and prevent the dispute from spreading. **A question that required an inquiry into property, evidence, and debt was therefore finally handled with morality.**

From this small dispute, one can see that Hai Rui was not facing an isolated judicial difficulty but a governing predicament shared by an enormous empire.

## An Empire Run by Morality and Documents

One summary in *The Year of Wanli* has stayed with me: politically, the empire was centralized; spiritually, it depended on morality; administratively, it used documents. The emperor’s authority was limitless in name, but the people who actually carried orders to the provinces were civil officials educated in the same classics and speaking the same moral language.

The civil service was originally created to solve a real problem.

In a vast agricultural society where the overwhelming majority of people were farmers, the center could not directly handle every village’s taxes, lawsuits, public order, and production. It needed a network connecting emperor, court, local officials, and rural society. The civil bureaucracy formed the body of that network. It selected people through the examinations, transmitted orders through paperwork, and relied on shared ethical ideas to sustain basic order in a vast “union of rural communities.”

That shared faith was not meaningless. Hai Rui shows that morality was not merely a hypocritical outer layer. Some people were indeed willing to pay dearly for integrity, loyalty, and an ideal of life. If the civil bureaucracy had fully lived those beliefs, they could have become a formidable force.

But that was precisely where the problem began.

When morality ceased to be the boundary of governance and instead began to substitute for investigation, evidence, fiscal capacity, justice, and administrative technique, it was asked to perform work it could not perform. A missed tax target could be interpreted as an official’s failure of duty; local unrest could be attributed to bad character or failed moral instruction; opposition to policy could be packaged as a conflict between loyalty and treachery, good and evil, or pure and impure motives.

**Questions of fact, cost, and method were turned into judgments about people.**

The *Dao De Jing* says:

> “When the Great Way is abandoned, there are benevolence and righteousness; when cleverness appears, there is great falsity.”
>
> — *Dao De Jing*

The point is not that benevolence itself is false. Morality can sustain order where institutions are weak. But an excessive dependence on morality also produces moral performance: genuine conviction and false packaging begin to use precisely the same vocabulary. **The system damages not only state capacity, but also the moral language on which it depends.**

## The Two Forces within the Civil Bureaucracy

Civil officials shared a classical education and political ideals, yet beneath that shared faith each had a real life of his own.

In a society of relatively fixed social ranks, passing the examinations and entering office was one of the principal ways to cross class boundaries, accumulate wealth, and change a family’s fate. A man’s degree was usually not his achievement alone; behind it stood the thrift of fathers and grandfathers, the support of wives and mothers, and years of investment by an entire family.

Once he acquired power, improving his family’s life, caring for relatives, and arranging positions for people from his native place could therefore seem almost a matter of course. Official salaries also fell short of actual living expenses, and gray income became increasingly normalized. Public moral standards and practical ways of living thus coexisted in the same person.

Beyond individual interests, the bureaucracy was tied together by complicated networks of examination cohorts, native places, teachers and students, and marriage. To offend one person could mean offending the people behind him; one personnel adjustment could unsettle an extensive web of interests. Everyone spoke of loyalty, honor, and the public good. Everyone also calculated position, prospects, and family.

The civil bureaucracy was not a simply hypocritical group, but a more complex compound: it contained the upward force of shared belief and the fractures created by private interests; it required cooperation while constantly producing mutual restraint. Beneath a coarse moral exterior, it maintained a fragile and temporary balance.

## How Far Could This System Actually Govern?

That balance did not give the empire a truly detailed capacity to govern.

The center seemed to hold everything, but its ability to reach deeply into local society was limited. Many village affairs still depended on gentry, elders, and local relationships. In civil disputes, the government lacked adequate procedures for investigation, evidence, and defense; in assessing officials, it lacked a stable and detailed standard of performance, often looking only at whether taxes arrived and whether disorder broke out.

The governing aim of the civil service was limited as well. Its first purpose was never the sustained improvement of people’s material welfare, nor wealth and military strength, nor an expanding national capacity in finance, commerce, and war. It sought to keep society stable at a minimum material level and thereby allow dynastic rule to continue.

To use the language of my original notes: a good year did not need to make everyone full; a bad year only had to avoid large numbers “filling the ditches.” That was already the standard of success. Such a system did not treat continuous development or outward expansion as its main purpose. Over time its form hardened: room for external and horizontal growth narrowed, while struggle over office, reputation, interest, and power became more intense within.

Finance was no different. The state lacked a unified and clear system for collection, transport, and expenditure. One place often supplied another directly. Avoiding a single centralized transfer point reduced some movement, but it also made local levies, exemptions, and rent-seeking extraordinarily complicated. The center was highly centralized in name, but not necessarily able to deploy resources flexibly.

Currency, lending, and land were intertwined. Commercial rules were unstable; high-interest loans often used land as collateral, and lending became a force behind land concentration. It is difficult here to draw a simple line between landlord and farmer, exploiter and exploited. One person might be exploited in one relation and lend surplus money at a high rate in another.

In military affairs, the limits of this way of governing became even clearer. Generals were constrained by civil officials and provincial governors. Logistics depended on scattered local finance; weapons production lacked a unified system; military command was divided. The empire did not entirely lack troops, arms, or able commanders. It lacked the capacity to organize those forces continuously.

The state created the civil bureaucracy to solve problems, yet in the end many problems had their roots in the bureaucracy as well. It connected center and locality, yet also limited both. It used morality to sustain order, yet consumed morality in political struggle. It helped society recover early in the dynasty, and later found itself increasingly unable to govern an empire that had grown more complex.

This was the world shared by Zhang Juzheng, Shen Shixing, the Wanli Emperor, Hai Rui, Qi Jiguang, and Li Zhi. They are not simply separate biographies. Each occupied a different position in the same net, chose a different response, and was pulled back by it.

## Three Ways of Handling a Powder Keg

The civil bureaucracy relied on shared moral belief to preserve its unity, but inside it were personal interests, factional relationships, and struggles for power. It was both a hardened compound and a powder keg held together only with difficulty.

Zhang Juzheng, Shen Shixing, and the Wanli Emperor all faced that same problem. Each saw the bureaucracy’s complexity and division, yet chose a different way to handle it: Zhang tried to remake it through force; Shen hoped to preserve it through moderation; Wanli eventually chose no longer to cooperate with it.

### Zhang Juzheng: Reform Could Begin Only with Personal Authority

Zhang Juzheng was unquestionably intelligent and energetic. He was skilled in both administration and personnel, and could make many things run in accordance with his will. He did not merely want to preserve the status quo. He had a political ideal: to enlarge state revenue and strengthen the country. This was why he promoted a nationwide land survey and sought to reorganize taxation and finance.

But what he faced was not a national machine capable of executing commands precisely.

The empire lacked complete fiscal, monetary, and judicial institutions. The center’s grip on localities was limited, and what happened to a policy after it left the court depended heavily on officials at every level. If Zhang wanted reform, he could not merely issue an order. He had to adjust personnel and put people he trusted—people capable of carrying policy through—into key positions.

His intervention in appointments, promotion of allies, and suppression of opponents were therefore not simply expressions of personal ambition. They were among the limited instruments that reform could use under those conditions. The more reform required execution, the more it required concentrated authority; the more concentrated authority became, the more it disrupted the existing balance within the civil bureaucracy.

After Zhang’s death, he was purged for interfering in personnel, appointing his own men, and dominating the court. In my view, this was precisely the kind of political means a reformer needed in order to pursue a political program. His opponents’ attacks did not necessarily arise from indignation at injustice alone. A more immediate reason may have been that they had not gained the place they expected in the distribution of power and interests under Zhang.

The bureaucracy contained dense networks of classmates, native-place ties, teachers and students, and marriage. To change one official’s position affected not one person but a whole group’s interests and sense of security. Zhang’s effort to build a personnel network capable of executing reform inevitably antagonized many officials in older networks.

Yet those political conflicts could not appear openly as conflicts of interest. The bureaucracy spoke in moral language; the counterattack on Zhang therefore had to become a moral trial. His luxurious life, acceptance of gifts, and favoritism in appointments became points of entry for the purge.

This does not mean those issues were imaginary. It means they were not the whole reason for opposition. Corruption was not rare in the bureaucracy, and Zhang’s accusers were not necessarily more upright. Once the political balance had been broken, blemishes in personal conduct could become the most powerful weapon.

My overall judgment of Zhang remains positive. During the decade when he dominated government, Ming political and economic life at least showed an upward tendency. His land survey and tax reforms also restrained the civil bureaucracy as a whole. Compared with an honest, upright official who accomplishes nothing, the fact that his measures produced real effects matters more.

It would also be too simple to say that Zhang cared only about particular events and ignored institution-building. The land survey itself was a foundation for fiscal and tax reform. He did not lack the intention to create a new system; these tasks were simply unfinished when he died. The end of one life became the end of a reform process.

**Zhang’s experience formed a cycle that was almost impossible to escape: a state without mature institutions and tools of execution had to rely on personal authority; personal authority had to work through appointments; appointments broke the bureaucracy’s balance of interests; the broken balance then struck back through moral judgment; reformer and reform were purged together.**

Through personal strength, Zhang temporarily moved the immense system. But because reform was tied too closely to one person’s fate, the two were ultimately met by the same backlash.

### Shen Shixing: Wrapping the Contradiction Again

Shen Shixing was Zhang Juzheng’s student, but he did not choose to continue his teacher’s method.

He understood more clearly the tangled relations inside the bureaucracy. Every official had interests, a family, fellow graduates, and fellow provincials; he also understood that drastic personnel changes gave the whole group a powerful sense of insecurity. Rather than break the balance again by force, he tried to lower the temperature of conflict and mend fractures between factions.

Where Zhang relied on personal authority and appointments, Shen relied more on the moral principles shared by the bureaucracy. He repeatedly emphasized sincerity, loyalty, and common political ideals, hoping that under the pull of common values people would temporarily set aside their competing interests.

This was not without value.

The bureaucracy could still hold together not only because of power, but because its members shared a classical education and moral faith. Shen tried to summon those common elements again, allowing divided officials to continue working in the same language. In the fiercely reactive politics after Zhang’s death, this moderation did have a buffering effect.

But it could only wrap the contradictions again; it could not remove their source.

Factional relations did not change. Conflicts over personnel and interests remained, and fiscal, judicial, and local governing capacity were not fundamentally improved. Everyone continued to pursue practical interests beneath moral language. So long as the balance was not openly broken, the surface could remain calm; when a new political event occurred, the old contradictions appeared again.

Shen hoped to serve as a mediator among all sides, but no one in such a system could remain forever outside it. Previous chief grand secretaries had often been ruined in political struggle. His eventual retirement home was already a comparatively fortunate ending.

Zhang tried to take the powder keg apart and rearrange its structure. Shen carefully retied what had already become loose. He preserved a temporary stability, but could not prevent the internal cracks from widening.

### The Wanli Emperor: Nonviolent Non-Cooperation

The Wanli Emperor faced the bureaucracy in an entirely different way.

Early in his reign, he was not uninterested in affairs from the beginning. He wanted diligent government, cared about military training, rain prayers, and other matters of state, and hoped to exercise political judgment. But the purge after Zhang Juzheng’s death and the bureaucracy’s ceaseless accusations and denunciations gradually disappointed him in both the ruler-minister relationship and the administrative system as a whole.

The emperor possessed supreme power in name, while the daily operators of imperial politics were civil officials. They needed the emperor to perform ritual and symbolic functions, to serve as the highest representative of moral order, but they did not want his personal will to enter practical politics too deeply.

In the book’s phrase, they needed “a living ancestor.”

The emperor could sacrifice to Heaven, plow ceremonially, receive his ministers’ homage, and become the symbol of political order. But when he tried to act according to his own will in succession, personnel, or government, he faced strong constraints from the bureaucracy. He appeared supreme, but possessed little room to exercise individual character or political ability freely.

Wanli could neither remake the civil bureaucracy nor accept entirely the role it assigned to him. He therefore chose a passive form of resistance—what I called in the original draft “nonviolent non-cooperation.”

The emperor had few instruments with which to move the bureaucracy. Besides appointments, there were honors, titles, and ceremonial rewards. Though symbolic, these were not empty. A group sustained by honor, reputation, and moral ideals also needed promotion and recognition to affirm its members’ worth.

By delaying appointments, reducing participation, and refusing cooperation over a long period, Wanli effectively cut off the bureaucracy’s paths of promotion and positive incentive. The side of the civil system that once possessed a capacity to inspire was gradually weakened; the factional and interest conflicts accumulated inside it did not disappear.

Those conflicts were shut into an environment with no outlet and could only tear at one another more fiercely. What Shen Shixing feared—rupture above and below, estrangement at the center and in the provinces—gradually became reality.

Wanli’s refusal to cooperate did not destroy the bureaucracy, but it left it increasingly without a common direction. He expressed his will through inaction; the bureaucracy continued to divide without imperial adjustment or incentive. Neither emperor nor ministers became victors.

Zhang broke the balance by force, Shen preserved it through moderation, and Wanli withdrew his support for it. They used three utterly different methods, yet none found a road outside the civil bureaucracy.

This was not because the three men made the same mistake. The tools available to them had been produced inside the system itself. Reform, mediation, and refusal to cooperate could only occur inside the range that system had drawn.

## Two Scalpel Cuts into an Empire

Zhang, Shen, and Wanli display the political difficulties and power relations within the bureaucracy. With Hai Rui and Qi Jiguang, the book’s attention moves from the court into the detailed workings of the empire.

Hai Rui was a scalpel cutting in through civil administration, justice, and finance; Qi Jiguang cut in through troops, weapons, and organizational technique. One put the moral standard proclaimed by the bureaucracy into practice to an extreme; the other tried to exercise professional ability within what reality allowed. Their paths seem wholly different, but they reached the boundary of the same system.

### Hai Rui: When a Person Lives by the Moral Standard

Hai Rui first demonstrates that the moral ideal shared by the civil bureaucracy was not wholly false.

In a bureaucratic world where loyalty, honor, and incorruptibility were constantly on people’s lips, Hai Rui genuinely arranged his life around those standards. He did not merely demand morality from others; he imposed it on himself. He lived simply, refused to accommodate the powerful, and could risk dismissal or death for principles he believed in.

If the bureaucracy as a whole could have acted as Hai Rui did, shared moral faith would indeed have had great force. Morality can lead people beyond short-term personal interest and make them willing to pay for an abstract political ideal. In this sense Hai Rui was not outside the civil system. He was one of its most nearly flawless products by its own standards.

Strangely, when such a person truly appeared, the official world felt uneasy.

Other officials used the same moral language, but they could not live by Hai Rui’s standard. Office was not only a route to political ideals; it was a route to changing personal and family fate. An official had parents, wife, children, and an entire family behind him. Daily expenses, social obligations, and a career could not be sustained by a meager salary alone.

Most people therefore had to strike a balance between public moral requirements and practical life. Hai Rui broke that balance. The more incorruptible he was, the more clearly everyone around him seemed insufficiently so; the more earnestly he practiced morality, the harder it became to explain the gray areas that official life had come to accept and rationalize.

Hai Rui’s presence was thus like an overly bright mirror. He did not have to accuse everyone; he made everyone able to see his own contradiction.

But Hai Rui’s deeper difficulty was not only his failure to fit into officialdom. It was that individual morality could not carry public governance by itself.

### Institutional Failure Seen through Hai Rui

The Ming official system contained sharp differences of rank. Civil officials, military officers, and clerks all handled public affairs, but they possessed very different status and paths of advancement.

Examination-trained civil officials had a relatively complete route upward and controlled the evaluation of others. Many clerks and technical personnel who handled concrete work had low status and limited salaries, and could hardly gain political standing through professional ability. Military officers too were evaluated and constrained by civil officials.

The system cared less about how much professional knowledge a person had in finance, justice, military affairs, or administration than about whether he matched the bureaucracy’s image of a “qualified official.” That image was moral before it was technical.

The local affairs Hai Rui handled, however, were all intensely concrete.

Land was dispersed; property and debt relations were complicated. Private lending often used land as collateral. In a good year, some households lent their surplus to others; in a bad year, debtors could not repay and land might pass to creditors. Land concentration did not always occur between two clearly opposed classes. It could run through the whole rural society. The same person could be exploited in one relation and charge high interest to someone poorer in another.

The state lacked both the people and resources to investigate these relations and a judicial system capable of handling complex property and contract claims. A magistrate might confront generations of transfers, kinship claims, and debts, yet could only decide with limited documents, statements by the parties, and his own moral judgment.

Hai Rui hoped to clear up such matters according to his sense of justice. But the more seriously he applied that sense, the more he touched the interest structure of local society. Land, lending, taxes, and families were intertwined; the line between complete good and complete evil was hard to draw. A judgment that seemed upright might protect one party while injuring another’s long-established claim.

This is not to say Hai Rui’s integrity had no value. It is to say that the problems before him exceeded the range personal integrity could resolve.

He could refuse a bribe, but could not create a property-rights system through honesty. He could judge impartially, but could not replace investigation, evidence, and judicial procedure through uprightness. He could require officials to take responsibility, but could not change the structure of finance, salaries, and local governance by morality alone.

**What should have been handled by technique and institutions was finally handed to morality; when morality could not bear it, failure was again explained as a personal moral failure. That is the deepest contradiction revealed by Hai Rui.**

He was the system’s most sincere practitioner, and by that sincerity made clearest what the system itself could not accomplish.

### Disorder in Fiscal Circulation

The local governance Hai Rui faced was linked to a disordered fiscal system across the empire.

The state had no system capable of collecting money and grain in a unified manner and redistributing them according to actual need. Many materials were supplied directly by one locality to another, while officials’ travel, transport, and public activities were often paid for through temporary local levies.

This arrangement appeared to avoid moving all wealth through the center, but it made fiscal relations countless and tangled. How much a place should bear, where it should send resources, and when it should finish were often without clear and uniform standards.

Families with political power could use relationships to obtain exemptions; smaller landholders and ordinary farmers without protection bore more of the burden. Local officials and intermediaries also possessed many opportunities to adjust, withhold, and seek rents.

The center was highly centralized in name, yet could not accurately know local wealth or move resources quickly according to need. Localities were formally subject to the center, yet their detailed execution was influenced by multiple offices and overlapping relations.

Weak fiscal capacity further limited administrative capacity. Official salaries were low, the treasury empty, and popular burdens heavy. Gray income and local levies followed. In the end, problems created by institutional gaps were again explained as official greed, local corruption, or people insufficiently taught.

**Much of what is described as moral evil can also be understood as a result of institutional incapacity.**

### Qi Jiguang: Making the Advanced Adapt to the Backward

If Hai Rui reveals imperial incapacity through civil administration and justice, Qi Jiguang opens the military system.

The Ming state possessed a large army in name, arsenals, shipyards, and many kinds of firearms. But none of this had been organized into a stable and efficient military system.

Army supply depended on scattered local contributions. The center lacked a unified capacity to allocate and command resources. When a regional force needed provisions and equipment, it often relied on the finance of designated places. Local fiscal relations were already disordered, so military logistics could hardly remain stable.

Military command was also constrained by provincial governors and civil officials. The court feared generals accumulating too much concentrated power and becoming something like regional warlords, so it split authority among different people. This could prevent one commander from becoming too strong, but it also made unified military command difficult.

The civil bureaucracy’s understanding of commanders was limited. It more readily judged a man by physical strength or martial skill than by military organization, strategy, logistics, and technology. Even a military officer who built real merit found it difficult to secure a stable income or standing after retirement.

Weapons production was limited by the same organizational form. Ming arsenals could be large collections of workshops, but they did not form a unified and stable production chain. Weapons from different workshops varied greatly in quality, and recorded quantities could diverge sharply from reality. Technology existed, yet the organization could not turn it into a sustained and reproducible military capacity.

Qi Jiguang’s value lay in knowing both military technique and the environment in which he lived.

He did not propose a grand plan to remake the whole Ming military system, because even if he had, it could not have been carried out. Within what was possible, he trained troops, improved tactics, organized soldiers, and compressed his military ideas into a form the system could accept.

In my view, he was “making the advanced adapt to the backward.”

That adaptation was not a simple surrender. Qi knew that advanced ideas alone were not enough. If they could not find a way to live inside the existing environment, even correct military thinking could not become real force. He therefore cooperated with provincial officials and accepted the political rules of the civil system. He did not seek to build an ideal modern army all at once; within the space reality allowed, he developed his ability as far as possible.

This realism was one reason Qi achieved what he did.

But his success also depended heavily on a particular political environment. He needed local civil support, fiscal and logistical cooperation, and later Zhang Juzheng’s appreciation and protection. Military achievement seemed to belong to Qi alone, but a fragile political relationship always provided the room for it.

When Zhang died and was purged, Qi immediately lost his most important protection. The political relationships that had enabled his talent also became the source of backlash when they changed.

This does not make his past military achievements false. It shows that professional ability could not acquire an independent status in this system. Military technique had to depend on political protection in order to exist; once that protection disappeared, technique and merit were not enough to secure a general’s position.

### Flaws in Conduct and the Wholeness of a Historical Figure

The book does not turn Qi Jiguang into a spotless hero.

It records elements of his family life, personal enjoyment, and the receipt of gifts. These details do not negate his military achievement. They make him a more complete historical figure.

Qi did not, like Hai Rui, practice the moral standards proclaimed by the bureaucracy to their farthest extreme. He accepted practical interest and a degree of compromise. He did not make military ability simply a tool for advancement and wealth, but neither did he refuse the pleasures of personal life.

This imperfection in conduct does not, in the author’s view, determine his total historical value. Whether every detail of a person’s life meets the highest moral demand and whether he built an effective army are questions on different levels.

This is one of the distinctive ways *The Year of Wanli* looks at people. It does not use one moral standard to decide a person’s entire value. Hai Rui was nearly unimpeachable morally, yet struggled to achieve effect in practical politics. Qi had practical compromises and personal flaws, yet accomplished what many cleaner people could not.

They are not a positive and a negative type. They illuminate the same empire from two directions.

### The Boundary Reached by Both Morality and Technique

Hai Rui and Qi Jiguang took wholly different paths.

Hai Rui believed in morality and carried that belief to an extreme. He refused to compromise with reality and became an outsider in officialdom.

Qi believed in technique, but understood that technique had to find a way to live in its actual environment. He was willing to compromise and cooperate with existing political relations, and was therefore able to realize his ambitions within a limited range.

Yet both endings show that the system could neither truly accommodate pure morality nor allow professional technique to develop independently.

Hai Rui’s morality was too pure for the routine balance of official life to continue; Qi’s military ability was too prominent for the established relation between civil and military power. When someone went too far in any direction—morality, technique, or military strength—the balance on which the system relied was disturbed.

**Between new technology and an old organization, only two outcomes are possible: advanced technology changes the backward organization, or a backward but tightly organized system restrains advanced technology. In Ming China, the latter occurred.**

## Li Zhi: A Crack Left in the Literati’s Inner World

In history textbooks and local cultural publicity in Quanzhou, I first encountered Li Zhi as an intense opponent of traditional thought. He was often placed within late-Ming intellectual change and the “sprouts of capitalism,” described as a representative of new thought and new forces.

But Li Zhi in *The Year of Wanli* is not such a simple figure.

He did criticize the hypocrisy of traditional literati. He disliked officials who proclaimed loyalty, shame, and honor while pursuing interests in actual life. But what he criticized was chiefly the gap between words and conduct. He did not completely leave Confucian thought. His contempt for officials who said one thing and did another arose precisely because they did not practice the moral principles they professed.

This placed Li Zhi in a contradictory position. He criticized officials and gentry while depending on their support for his life. He could see the gap between other people’s thought and behavior, yet could not make all his own actions fully consistent with his ideas. He resisted old standards while still using them; he resented moral constraint imposed from outside while not completely escaping the same judgments of good and evil within himself.

In the end, he turned to more indeterminate Buddhist expressions, trying to transcend the opposition between good and evil. This did not create a new intellectual system. It was more like a spiritual settlement he sought in a contradiction he could not reconcile.

### He Was Not a New Thinker Appearing from Nowhere

If we read Li Zhi through the labels later textbooks gave him, we easily expect that, since he criticized tradition, he should also have proposed a new theory capable of replacing it.

I think that asks too much of him.

Ideas do not arise merely because one person is intelligent or brave. They require corresponding economic forms, social relations, and real events. In Li Zhi’s era, older social structures still had great force, while new social relations could not support a wholly different framework of thought. It is difficult to imagine someone leaving behind his language, life, and material conditions to produce a complete theory beyond his age from nothing.

Li Zhi’s failure to construct a new system therefore does not make his criticism meaningless. What matters is not that he supplied an answer, but that he exposed the spiritual contradiction of the literati of his time:

> Between the classics they read, the moral standards they proclaimed publicly, and the way they actually lived, there was always a gulf that could not be bridged.

### A Widespread and Real Spiritual Pain

The civil bureaucracy could connect a vast empire precisely because its members shared a similar classical education. Whatever their origins, once they entered office through the examinations, they could use the same language of loyalty, honor, benevolence, righteousness, and shame to discuss public affairs.

This common faith had real force. Even without a rigorous technical system, it allowed the bureaucracy to maintain basic standards of value; it also made someone like Hai Rui willing to pay a great personal price for principle.

But when the same moral standard was used to explain politics, administration, interests, and private life alike, it became a unifying and exclusive constraint.

Real life contains complicated interests, emotions, family responsibilities, and needs of survival, yet it could only be judged through one vocabulary of good and evil. Officials were supposed to be loyal, incorruptible, and self-restrained; but office also had the practical function of changing a family’s fate, caring for relatives, and accumulating wealth.

Someone who lived completely by the highest moral standard could become an outsider like Hai Rui: above reproach, but not accommodated by ordinary official relationships. Someone who lived according to practical relationships and interests still had to explain himself in moral terms.

Most people could only speak one moral language in public, live by another set of rules in practice, and then find reasons to make the two fit together. Li Zhi differed in that he could not simply look away from this condition.

## Epilogue: When an Institution Reaches Its Opposite

The Ming China depicted in *The Year of Wanli* was, in essence, a vast rural union in which more than ninety percent of people worked in agriculture. Its territory was immense, its population large, and its local products, customs, and social relations diverse, while the governing apparatus created by its rulers remained simple and coarse. It lacked precise fiscal statistics, detailed property institutions, and organizational resources capable of entering the grassroots and verifying facts. At the same time, power was highly concentrated at the center, leaving localities little space to adjust to real conditions on their own.

Such a system did not possess a genuine capacity for management in the modern sense. It could not reach every corner of society or precisely handle each particular matter. The role of the civil bureaucracy emerged in those gaps of governing capacity.

Shared classical education unified thought; moral names constrained conduct; personal relations, reputation, and informal connections coordinated official institutions; gentry and lineages carried central will to the local level. Early in the dynasty, this low-cost way of governing could indeed preserve order and support economic and social development. A vast agricultural state could remain unified for a long time without an excessively complicated administration.

But an institution’s function is never fixed.

As population grew, land relations became complex, and fiscal and military affairs expanded, problems that could once be managed through moral consensus and interpersonal adjustment began to require professional techniques, precise institutions, and stronger organizational capacities. The bureaucracy, however, had already become tightly bound to the old political structure. It had once filled institutional gaps; later it began to prevent those gaps from being replaced by genuine institution-building.

It preserved order while excluding change; mediated contradictions while concealing them; used morality to maintain a common language in the bureaucracy while continually translating fiscal, military, and judicial questions into questions of character. In the end, the forces on which the civil system had succeeded became the forces that limited its further development.

The *Dao De Jing* says:

> “To hold and fill it is not as good as to stop.”
>
> — *Dao De Jing*

As an institution matures, it also forms inertia. The more it relies on established means to solve problems, the harder it is to recognize that those means no longer fit. The bureaucracy did not suddenly become corrupt, nor did it one day collectively lose its sense of responsibility. Many people continued sincerely to maintain the order as they understood it. Yet when everyone was trying his best within old rules, the rules themselves were becoming less and less able to meet reality.

This makes Ming decline especially complex. Zhang tried to change the system and had to depend on personal authority; Shen tried to sustain it and could only repeatedly patch the surface balance; Wanli refused the role the system prescribed and opposed the bureaucracy passively; Hai Rui carried morality almost to an extreme but could not solve what lay beyond morality; Qi built an effective military method but had to make advanced technique adapt to a backward organization; Li Zhi tried to escape the literati’s spiritual order and never fully left it.

Their fates cannot be reduced to loyalty or treachery, courage or weakness. Individual character mattered, of course. But the paths they could take, the tools they could use, and the price they finally paid had already been bounded, in broad outline, by the system itself.

There is value in studying such a process of decline. We now live in a world community marked by technological development and many possible paths of civilization. Observing the end and decline of an earlier civilization can offer a useful feeling for how different roads of development are possible. It does not supply a ready-made answer.

This is what moves me most in Huang Renyu’s “macro-history.” It asks us to look more broadly and over a longer span at people, ourselves, and society. We can make calmer distinctions: which questions belong to morality, and which to technique and institutions? Which belong to an individual, and which to organization and society?

**To resist a crude, one-dimensional attribution is not to deny moral responsibility. It is to recognize that morality cannot replace technique, and that personal character alone cannot explain an organization or a society.**

History may not yield a closed conclusion. It may simply make conclusions that are too easy begin to feel less convincing.
