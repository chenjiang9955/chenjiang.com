*Reading The Year of Wanli as the story of a bureaucracy that gradually exhausted both morality and its own capacity to govern.*

## Begin With a Dispute Over a Field

If I had to reduce the enormous problems of *The Year of Wanli* to one small, concrete scene, I would begin with the land disputes that Hai Rui had to confront.

A field may look like a few humble acres. Behind it, however, may lie the livelihood of several generations. Rural land was scattered, and ownership was often tangled. Between good harvests and bad ones came loans, pawns, mortgages, and transfers. A single contract could rarely settle the whole story. Who first bought the land? Was it divided when the family split? Had it been mortgaged in a famine? Who received the income, and who carried the debt? The answer might be hidden in the long memory of a household.

The government had little organizational capacity to investigate such facts. It had nothing like modern registration of ownership, fact-finding, rules of evidence, or a real process of rebuttal. Law was not especially concerned with whether every single case reached precise fairness. Its deeper concern was whether the village, and the social order around it, could remain stable. So when an official faced a dispute involving contracts, property, evidence, and investigation, the most familiar instrument left in his hands was morality.

Who appeared more dutiful? Who had a reputation for filial conduct? Whose claim might disturb village peace? Who ought to yield? Such judgments could sometimes stop a quarrel, but they could not replace the work of establishing facts. Morality ceased to be only a boundary. It became an instrument of adjudication.

**This is the strongest impression I take from rereading *The Year of Wanli*: the Ming problem was not that no one cared about morality. It was that morality was asked to do too much work that should have been done by institutions, techniques, and administrative capacity. Morality was raised very high, used too often, and damaged in both directions.**

> “When the great Way is abandoned, benevolence and righteousness appear; when cleverness arises, great falseness follows.”
>
> — *Dao De Jing*

Read here, this does not mean benevolence itself is false. It suggests something else: when a society must use dense moral language to fill the gaps of governance, morality is drawn into endless concrete interests. Facts that need to be clarified become judgments of character; institutions that need adjustment become contests between loyalty and treachery.

## How Far Could This System Actually Govern?

Huang Renyu gives a powerful summary of Ming politics: the empire was centralized in its political structure, supported by morality in its spiritual life, and administered through paperwork - that is, through a civil bureaucracy.

The bureaucracy was not simply a collection of hypocrites. Shared education in the classics, ritual order, and moral ideals truly connected its members. They gave people who had never met a common language for loyalty, reputation, responsibility, and order. They also led some people to pay a high personal price for their convictions. Hai Rui is moving precisely because he shows that this faith was not empty speech.

But the civil service did not live outside material desire. In a society with relatively fixed social layers, the examination system and official office were among the few reliable ways for a family to gain status, wealth, and mobility. One man’s success in the examinations often rested on the thrift of his parents and the labor of his wife and mother. Once he acquired power, improving his family’s life, caring for relatives, and arranging opportunities for protégés could easily seem part of the ordinary meaning of success. Salaries were low, while local administration involved many costs that official pay did not cover. Informal income therefore became a reality everyone understood beneath the language of moral duty.

Shared belief did not erase interests. It became entangled with networks of native-place ties, examination cohorts, marriage, teachers, students, and former colleagues. To offend one person could mean offending a whole cluster of people. A single appointment could unsettle an entire web of relationships. The bureaucracy needed moral language to preserve its dignity, while using that dignity to manage very concrete interests.

Its deeper limitation was the narrowness of its actual reach into society. In a country where more than 90 percent of the population farmed and many could not read, central documents truly reached mainly literati, officials, and local gentry. Much of village life was governed through elders, lineages, local notables, and abstract moral principles. Finance, currency, land, justice, and the military all lacked the technical and organizational conditions for fine-grained management.

**The purpose of this system was never chiefly to raise welfare, make the country wealthy and militarily powerful, or expand national capacity. It sought to preserve social stability and the continuity of rule on the lowest material foundation possible. A good year did not have to make everyone well-fed; a bad year merely had to avoid masses dying in the ditches. Holding that line was already treated as success.**

This goal left little impulse for outward expansion, horizontal development, or self-renewal. The institutional form gradually hardened, while competition for office, prestige, interest, and power grew more intense within it. The civil bureaucracy had once supported economic and social recovery. Later, it increasingly resembled a difficult powder keg.

## Three Ways of Handling a Powder Keg

One of the pleasures of *The Year of Wanli* is that it does not discuss institutions in the abstract. It lets different people step into the powder keg one after another.

### Zhang Juzheng: Why Reform Turns Back on the Reformer

Zhang Juzheng was unquestionably a man of exceptional ability. He had energy, political judgment, and a desire to improve finance, land administration, and the strength of the state. His nationwide land survey and fiscal reorganization were not merely responses to isolated events. They were an attempt to find a route through a fiscal order that was already becoming strained.

Yet in an environment with weak institutions and weak technical capacity, the means available to reform were extremely limited. There was no mature mechanism to ensure that directives would be carried out, nor a stable system to coordinate resistance in the provinces. Reform therefore had to rely on personal authority and personnel decisions: promoting those who could carry a program forward and removing those who opposed it. Control of appointments was not simply Zhang’s personal preference. It was a necessary political means of making reform happen.

The problem lay precisely there. An appointment did not affect only one post; it disturbed a field of interests and relationships. The more Zhang tried to push reform, the harder it became to avoid breaking the existing balance within the bureaucracy. Once that balance was disturbed, political conflict could quickly be translated into moral accusation. His opponents were not necessarily defending principle alone; they had interests of their own. Zhang’s luxury, conduct, and use of personnel therefore became entry points for a wider reckoning with his reforms.

**The result was a cycle that was difficult to escape: when institutions and techniques are insufficient, reform relies on personal authority; personal authority works through appointments; appointments disturb group balances; the group’s backlash returns in the language of morality; the reformer and the reform are then struck together.**

Zhang died too early for many of his arrangements to become something more durable than his own authority. His ending does not erase what his reforms achieved. It shows how hard it was, in that system, to separate the fate of a reform from the fate of the person carrying it.

### Shen Shixing: Covering a Crack Is Not Removing It

Shen Shixing chose differently. He understood more clearly the complexity within the civil bureaucracy, and he was more willing to acknowledge human interests, anxieties, and rivalries. He tried to make conflicts less intense, preserve dignity, and emphasize the shared morality, sincerity, and order in which the group still believed.

This was not useless. A vast bureaucracy cannot always be moved by force. Buffering, mediation, and balance are part of politics. But shared belief can cover a crack for a time; it cannot remove the source of the crack. Fiscal stress, insecurity over appointments, factional interests, and the distance between center and locality do not disappear simply because everyone speaks more often about “the larger good.”

Shen’s eventual ability to retire home was comparatively peaceful. Yet what he had preserved was still a situation slowly continuing to split.

### Wanli: Withdrawing Cooperation and Closing the Exit

The Wanli Emperor’s situation was even more tragic. Ming political theory placed the emperor at the highest point, while actual administration increasingly depended on the civil bureaucracy. What the officials needed from an emperor was closer to a ritual and moral center - a living ancestor. He was to provide name, ceremony, and ultimate endorsement, but was not always expected to show an active political will of his own.

Wanli was not without ambition in his early years. But through the conflicts that followed Zhang Juzheng’s death, he gradually found that he could neither truly command the bureaucracy nor easily decide appointments, military matters, or succession according to his own preference. In the end, he adopted something close to nonviolent non-cooperation: he stopped participating actively, delayed appointments, and withdrew the titles, honors, and ceremonial incentives that had helped sustain the system.

Once the few positive instruments in the emperor’s hands were removed, the bureaucracy also lost some of its brighter side: expectation of advancement, recognition of rank, and the imagination of a shared undertaking. What remained was a closed pond of offices and mutual struggle. Wanli did not destroy the bureaucracy, but he removed one of the outlets through which its own tensions might have been managed.

## Hai Rui and Qi Jiguang: The Detail of Imperial Incapacity

If Zhang Juzheng, Shen Shixing, and Wanli show three choices within the bureaucracy, Hai Rui and Qi Jiguang make its incapacity visible in detail.

Hai Rui confronted more than corrupt officials. In the bureaucratic order around him, civil officials, military officers, and clerks occupied unequal positions. Technical work lacked clear advancement and security. Salaries were low, and much ordinary administration had to be sustained by unofficial income. A system that demanded moral self-discipline while leaving people to solve daily livelihood and working costs outside formal arrangements gradually weakened its own moral standard through endless acts of self-explanation.

Finance, currency, land, and justice were more complicated still. High-interest lending, mortgages, and land concentration among ordinary people cannot be explained simply as greed. They were woven together with the weakness of currency, taxation, and property registration. Fiscal transfers between center and locality were often managed through multiple channels and repeated layers. This created opportunities for rent-seeking, but it also made administration slow and rigid.

Hai Rui could remain honest and judge cases with a personal sense of fairness. Yet personal integrity could not replace property boundaries, rules of evidence, investigative capacity, or judicial procedure. Problems requiring technique and institutions cannot finally be resolved by morality alone. Hai Rui matters because he lived the moral standards proclaimed by the civil service with unusual seriousness; his limits reveal why morality could not carry public governance by itself.

Qi Jiguang stood at the other end of the military system. He did not pursue an ideal detached from reality. Within what reality allowed, he trained troops, improved tactics, and organized coordination, making compromise with the environment part of the cost of realizing his aims. Yet the selection and appointment of commanders, weapons production, logistics, and the division of command were all constrained by the civil bureaucracy and local administration. Technique had no natural space in which to unfold.

Qi’s success therefore depended heavily on political protection. When Zhang Juzheng died and that protection vanished, military achievement alone could not easily withstand the backlash. **The book presents a hard proposition: instead of advanced technique overcoming a backward organization, a backward but tightly organized system can restrain advanced technique.** Under Ming conditions, the latter prevailed.

## Li Zhi: A Crack in the Inner World of the Literati

Li Zhi is often presented as a new thinker who appeared out of nowhere, as though he naturally represented an entirely different historical force. I do not read him that way.

His greatest force lies in his criticism of old thought and of the gap between what literati said and how they lived. He saw the distance between public moral standards and private life. He saw many educated people speak of benevolence and honor while struggling, in practice, with fame, wealth, and relationships.

But Li Zhi did not truly leave the framework of Confucian thought, nor could he create from nothing a new system of ideas capable of replacing it. Given the economic and social conditions of his time, asking him to become someone wholly beyond his age would be too severe. He still depended on the cultural world he criticized for language, identity, and livelihood, and he turned to Buddhism for a form of spiritual settlement.

That contradiction does not weaken his value. It makes visible a widespread and very real spiritual pain. When a society has only one highly unified moral language while human life remains so complicated, literati must move constantly between elevated public statements and their actual daily situation. The gap between words and actions is not always mere personal hypocrisy. It can also be the narrow space for existence left by a cultural system.

## Closing: Understanding History Through a Process of Decline

*The Year of Wanli* describes a vast rural union in which more than 90 percent of the population farmed. Its governing machinery was simple and coarse, yet it carried the demands of a highly centralized order. In the beginning, the civil bureaucracy filled the gaps in the system. Through shared education, personal networks, local gentry, and moral belief, it kept an enormous society functioning, however imperfectly.

As population, wealth, land relations, and fiscal and military affairs became more complex, the methods that had once sustained order also became obstacles to further development. The system had once gathered consensus; later it excluded difference. It had once mediated conflict; later it concealed conflict. It had once used morality to fill administrative gaps; later it asked morality to bear the weight of technique, institutions, and fact.

> “To hold and fill it is not as good as to stop.”
>
> — *Dao De Jing*

The more mature an institution becomes, the more likely it is to form its own inertia. It does not suddenly become corrupt on one particular day. Many people may still sincerely defend the order as they understand it. Yet when everyone is doing their best within old rules, the old rules themselves may no longer be able to meet reality.

There is value in studying such a process of decline. We live in a world community still being reshaped by rapid technological development and multiple civilizational paths. To observe how an earlier civilization formed, matured, and gradually lost vitality inside its own inertia is not to look for a ready-made plan for the present. It is to gain a different feeling for civilization, institutions, and the human condition.

This is what moves me most in Huang Renyu’s “macro-history.” When the scale of time is widened, many matters that seem to concern only character, morality, or individual choice reveal an organizational and institutional background. When the field of vision is widened, conflicts described as moral conflicts also reveal fiscal, technical, and social-structural causes.

**Morality remains morality, and individuals remain responsible for their choices. But morality cannot replace technique, and personal character cannot by itself explain how an organization or a society functions.** To distinguish moral questions from technical and institutional ones, and personal questions from organizational and social ones, seems closer to what *The Year of Wanli* leaves with me than any easy assignment of historical good and evil.

History may not answer for those who come after it. Perhaps its more important work is to make some answers that are too easy, too certain, slowly stop working.
