At thirty-six, I stepped down from a machine that had been spinning at high speed for twelve years, and suddenly I did not know where to go.

It was not that there were no options. There were many, so many that they made me anxious. The real question was: by what standard should I judge which option was right?

Return to real estate? The industry was declining, and friends warned me not to go back. Change fields? That meant starting from zero and abandoning twelve years of accumulated experience. Become a consultant? Without a platform behind me, why would anyone trust me? Take the civil service exam? I had already passed the age threshold.

Every option made sense. Every option also had serious flaws. After months of moving back and forth between them, I realized there was a deeper problem: I had been using external standards to evaluate my life - income, stability, status, family expectations - but I had never asked myself what kind of life I actually wanted.

## The Aftereffect of an Efficient Life

Working in real estate finance is, in essence, managing efficiency for capital. Every dollar must show a return. Every decision must answer to an indicator.

After twelve years, this way of thinking was no longer simply a tool I used. It had become part of who I was.

So when I faced my own life choices, my mind automatically used the same logic: return on investment, opportunity cost, risk exposure, time value. I evaluated life as if it were an investment project, then discovered that no project could pass my harsh financial model.

This is the aftereffect of an efficient life: you become very good at evaluating value, and forget how to define it.

## From "Should I?" to "What Do I Want?"

It took me a long time to understand this: in every major career decision I had made, I was asking the same question - is this the right choice?

Behind "right" stood an external evaluation system: how others would see it, how the industry would see it, how it would look on a resume, where it could lead next. These things matter, of course. But they cannot answer the more basic questions:

Are you happy? When you wake up and go to work, do you feel anticipation or endurance? How far is your daily work from the things you truly find meaningful?

When I changed the question from "what should I do?" to "what kind of life do I want?", things became much clearer.

What I wanted was simple: enough time to be alone and read, space to write what I wanted to write, the ability to slow down and eat a meal with people I care about, and not to spend the clearest hours of my day in meeting rooms.

In a sense, I needed very little. I had simply never allowed myself to want it.

## The Meaning of Waiting

The rhythm of the real estate industry is often inhuman. A project may take two to five years from land acquisition to delivery, but bosses want monthly reports, headquarters wants weekly reports, and regional teams want daily reports. Every level is urgent because every level is being urged from above.

After living in that rhythm for too long, a person forgets that waiting is normal.

Everything takes time: changing industries, understanding oneself, even resting.

It took me about a year to learn how to wait. Not passive, anxious waiting, but active waiting - allowing things to unfold. This kind of waiting is not doing nothing. It is doing things that may not produce immediate results but are truly important: reading, writing, exercising, accompanying people, staring into space.

In an age that worships speed, learning to wait is itself a form of resistance.

## Unfinished

The greatest gain of thirty-six was accepting that I did not know the answer.

I used to demand answers from myself: the next step, the optimal solution, the feeling that everything was under control. Now I can say calmly: I do not know. I do not know where I will go, what I am doing, or what the future will become.

Strangely, when you stop pretending to know everything, you become closer to what is real. Real life is uncertain, open, and without a standard answer.

This blog is probably born from that "not knowing." I do not write because I have answers. I write because the process of writing brings me a little closer to myself.

If you are also standing at a crossroads and do not know where to go - it is all right. I am too.

We can be on the road together.
